A NICU Nurse on What Your Newborn's Cry Actually Means

A NICU nurse and mom of three explains what a newborn's cry means, from pitch and crying frequency to the sounds that warrant a call tonight.

Words by Melissa Torres · November 17, 2026

A newborn's cry mostly tells you how urgent something feels to your baby. It rarely tells you exactly what's wrong, and the pitch and rhythm carry more information than any hunger-sound chart. I learned that on a night shift in our level III NICU in 2019. A 34-weeker in bay 6 started a thin, piercing cry that sat way above every other baby on the unit, and the charge nurse and I both turned our heads before any alarm went off. His blood sugar came back at 38. So my position is simple: learn your baby's normal sound and normal rhythm, and stop trying to translate every wail into a specific need.

I've also done this at home three times, with three very different criers. My oldest screamed like a smoke detector over a wet diaper. Her younger brother barely fussed at all, which honestly worried me more. And the youngest had a 6 p.m. meltdown so reliable you could set a clock by it. None of them came with a manual, and the exact sound was never the useful clue. What mattered was the change: higher than usual, weaker than usual, or crying at a time that didn't fit the pattern.

What the pitch of a cry actually tells you

Here's where "frequency" gets literal. A healthy term newborn's cry usually has a fundamental frequency somewhere around 350 to 600 Hz. That's the basic note under the noise. Researchers who study cry acoustics, including LaGasse, Neal and Lester's 2005 review of infant cry analysis, describe cries that jump well above 1,000 Hz, called hyperphonation, as a fairly consistent marker of a stressed or compromised nervous system. Nobody measures hertz in a living room. You can hear it, though... it sounds shrill, almost squeaky, like the cry is coming from the top of the throat instead of the chest.

Pain sounds different from fussing

A pain cry tends to start suddenly and at full volume. There's a long first wail, a pause where the baby seems to hold their breath, and then another wail. I've watched this pattern hundreds of times during heel sticks. Fussing builds. Pain arrives. If your baby goes from calm to a full-force, breath-holding scream in under a second, look for a cause: a hair wrapped tight around a toe, a scratchy clothing tag, a car seat buckle pinching a thigh.

Weak cries deserve more attention than loud ones

Parents worry about volume. I worry about the opposite. A baby who used to cry with some force and now gives a faint, moaning whimper, especially one who's also hard to wake, is telling you something more serious than a red-faced screamer ever does. How often is normal? The crying curve nobody warns you about

The other frequency is how often and how long. Crying climbs through the first weeks, peaks around six weeks, and eases off by three to four months. A 2017 meta-analysis in The Journal of Pediatrics by Wolke, Bilgin and Samara pooled data on close to 8,700 infants and found average fussing and crying of about two hours a day in the first six weeks, with colic showing up in roughly 17 to 25 percent of babies in those early weeks.

Two hours a day... and that's normal. The National Center on Shaken Baby Syndrome's Period of PURPLE Crying program built its whole message around this curve, and I think it's the most honest framing out there. Some crying has no fixable cause, and that isn't a parenting failure.

Reading the pattern instead of the sound

  • Hunger: starts as rooting, hand-sucking and short grunty fusses, then builds over a few minutes into rhythmic crying.
  • Tired: whiny and on-and-off, often with face-rubbing into your shoulder, worse if you keep playing.
  • Overstimulated: head turns away, back arches, and the crying gets harder when you add more bouncing.
  • Evening cluster crying: late afternoon to around 11 p.m., peaking near week six, often paired with cluster feeding.
  • Pain: sudden, loud, with that breath-holding pause.

Timing matters too. Newborns crying at the tail end of a sleep cycle often aren't fully awake, and scooping them up at the first squeak can wake them properly. If that sounds familiar, our piece on what normal newborn sleep cycles look like in week one covers how short those cycles really are.

Why I'm skeptical of cry translators and sound charts

There's a popular idea that every newborn makes the same handful of sounds, "neh" for hunger and so on, and that you can decode them from day one. The appeal is obvious. On hour three of rocking, a decoder ring sounds amazing. But the peer-reviewed support for universal cry "words" is thin, and in my experience parents who lean on it start second-guessing what's right in front of them. A baby rooting at your shirt is hungry regardless of the vowel. For more on this, see Newborn Sleep Cycles in Week One: What's Actually Normal (And What Isn't).

Apps are the newer version. My friend Dana downloaded one of those cry-analyzer apps with her second, and honestly... I wanted it to work, for her sake. Day one it told her he was "bored." He was nine days old. By the second night it said "hungry" while he was attached to her, mid-feed, crying because he'd lost the latch. She texted me the screenshot at 2:40 a.m. followed by a long string of laughing faces. I'm not 100% sure they're all that bad, and some research groups are doing serious work training models on cry audio. I just haven't seen one beat a tired parent who's been paying attention for a week.

Where the science does earn its keep is in the outliers. Acoustic research is good at flagging the abnormal cry, the shrill one or the weak one. It's much worse at separating "hungry" from "gassy" in a healthy baby, because those cries overlap heavily.

The cries that mean you call your pediatrician tonight

Most crying is loud and normal. A smaller set of changes means you pick up the phone, and I'd rather you make ten calls that turn out fine than sit on one that wasn't.

  • A cry that's suddenly high-pitched and shrill, and stays that way.
  • A weak or moaning cry, especially with a grunt on every breath.
  • Any crying with a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months. The American Academy of Pediatrics treats that as a call right away, no waiting to see.
  • Inconsolable crying for more than three hours that's new for your baby, or that comes with vomiting, a bulging soft spot or refused feeds.
  • A baby who's unusually hard to wake between cries.

Fever is where I see the most hesitation, often from parents who just came back from a family gathering and feel guilty about it. Getting outside isn't the problem. And if the crying is pushing you to your limit, lay the baby on their back in the crib and walk away for ten minutes. That's a safety plan, not a failure. Tonight, when your baby is fed and crying their ordinary everyday cry, record 20 seconds of it on your phone and label it "normal." That clip is your baseline. The next time something sounds off at 3 a.m., play it back and compare. If the new cry is clearly higher or weaker side by side, call your pediatrician and let them hear both recordings.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if my newborn is crying from pain?

A pain cry starts suddenly at full volume, with a long first wail and a pause where the baby seems to hold their breath. Check for a hair wrapped around a toe or finger, a scratchy tag or a pinching strap. If you find nothing and the crying won't settle, call your pediatrician.

How much crying is normal for a newborn?

Around two hours a day in the first six weeks is average, with a peak near six weeks and a clear drop by three to four months. Long evening crying spells are common in that window. New crying that lasts more than three hours, or comes with fever, vomiting or poor feeding, needs a call.

Does a high-pitched cry mean something is wrong with my baby?

A loud cry is usually fine, but a shrill, squeaky cry that's new and won't go away is different. It can come with low blood sugar, infection or a neurological problem. Call your pediatrician the same day, and sooner if your baby is also hard to wake or feeding poorly.
