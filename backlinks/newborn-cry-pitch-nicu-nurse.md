**Title:** NICU nurse here: stop trying to decode the cry, listen for the change instead

I've been a NICU nurse for years and I have three kids of my own. Here's what I wish someone had told me with my first: don't try to decode the cry like a dictionary. Those charts that say one sound means hungry and another means gas never held up for me, at work or at home.

What actually helps is listening to pitch and rhythm. A rhythmic cry that builds and then settles when you pick baby up or offer a feed is usually your baby saying 'something is uncomfortable, come help.' Most crying is like that. What I watch for is change. That means a cry that's suddenly much higher and thinner than your baby's normal, a weak or moaning cry, or crying that won't settle no matter what you try. Those are worth a call to your pediatrician or nurse line, especially if there's also a fever, poor feeding or unusual sleepiness.

You'll learn your own baby's baseline faster than you think. Trust that.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Can you tell what's wrong with a newborn by the sound of their cry?

Short answer: partly, but not the way most charts promise.

I'm a NICU nurse and a mom of three. After years of listening to babies on a level III unit, here's what I actually trust. A newborn's cry mostly tells you how urgent something feels to your baby. It rarely tells you exactly what's wrong. The 'hungry sound versus gassy sound' lists are appealing, but real cries overlap a lot. Context tells you more than the sound itself: when baby last ate, when you last changed the diaper, how long they've been awake.

What the cry does carry well is pitch and rhythm.

A typical cry is rhythmic. It builds, pauses for a breath and builds again. It usually eases when you respond with a feed, holding, burping, a diaper change or a calmer room. That's your baby's normal 'come help' signal, and it's most of newborn crying.

The cries I take seriously are the ones that change from that baseline:
- A sudden high-pitched, thin or piercing cry that doesn't sound like your baby
- A weak, whimpering or moaning cry
- Crying that won't settle for a long stretch, whatever you try
- Any unusual cry that comes with fever, poor feeding, fewer wet diapers, breathing that looks hard or unusual sleepiness

Those are reasons to call your pediatrician or nurse line, not to wait it out. For a baby under 3 months, a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher means calling right away.

If you want more detail, I wrote a longer piece about the NICU night that taught me this: https://siavashsed.github.io/newborniq/newborn-cry-pitch-nicu-nurse/

Your own ear is the best tool you have. Within a couple of weeks you'll know your baby's baseline, and noticing when it changes matters more than anything else.
