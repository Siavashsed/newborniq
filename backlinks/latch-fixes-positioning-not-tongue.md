**Title:** Before you book a tongue tie consult, check where your baby's hips are

Day two with my second, I was sure something was wrong with his mouth. Every latch hurt, he clicked, and I had already googled tongue tie three times before sunrise. What actually fixed it was embarrassingly simple: his body was in the wrong place.

A few things that changed everything for us:

- Hips and belly pulled in tight against me, not just his head turned toward me. If he has to twist his neck to reach, he can't open wide.
- Nose lined up with the nipple, not the mouth. He tips his head back to reach up, and that's what gets the wide gape.
- Waiting for a really wide open mouth before bringing him in, instead of rushing the moment he opened a little.
- Chin touching first, so more of the lower areola goes in.

Within a couple of feeds the pain dropped from toe-curling to manageable, and by the end of the week it was fine.

Not saying tongue ties aren't real, they are, and if pain sticks around after positioning changes an IBCLC is worth every penny. But it's worth ruling out positioning first.

**Hook:** Most painful breastfeeding latches are fixed by moving the baby's body, not treating the baby.

After years in the NICU and three babies of my own, the pattern is hard to miss. A parent arrives convinced something is wrong with their baby's tongue. Often, the real issue is a few inches of distance.

When a baby's hips drift away from the parent's body, the head has to turn and the chin tucks. A tucked chin means a shallow latch, and a shallow latch means pain.

The fix takes seconds: bring the hips in close, line the nose up with the nipple, and wait for a wide open mouth before bringing the baby in. No shield, no cream, no referral.

Tongue ties are real and deserve proper assessment when pain persists. But positioning should be the first thing we check, not the last.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why does breastfeeding hurt even though my baby seems to latch, and does it mean my baby has a tongue tie?

I'm a NICU nurse and a mother of three, and I've been on both sides of this. With my second, Mateo, I was in tears at 3 a.m. on day two, sure he had a tongue tie. He didn't. His body was just in the wrong place.

Pain with a baby who seems latched usually means the latch is shallow: the nipple sits near the front of the mouth, where the hard palate compresses it, instead of deep at the back near the soft palate. And a shallow latch is most often caused by positioning, not anatomy.

Here's what I'd check first, in this order:

1. Hips close. Your baby's belly should face and touch your body. If only the head is turned toward you, the neck twists and the jaw can't open fully.
2. Nose to nipple. Start with the nipple pointed at the nose, not the lips. Your baby tilts the head back to reach, which opens the mouth wider.
3. Wait for the gape. Bring the baby in only when the mouth is wide, like a yawn. Rushing a half-open mouth is the most common mistake I see.
4. Chin first. The chin and lower lip should land well below the nipple, so the latch is off-center with more of the lower areola inside.

Give each change a few feeds. If you're still in pain after that, your nipple comes out creased or lipstick-shaped, or your baby isn't gaining well, see an IBCLC or your pediatrician. Tongue ties are real, and a proper oral assessment matters. They're just not the first explanation.
