## QUORA
**Answer this question:** When can I safely give my newborn a pacifier without hurting breastfeeding?

As a NICU nurse and mother of three, I've answered this question for hundreds of families, and I've also lived the messy version of it myself.

The short answer: wait until breastfeeding is going well, which for most babies is around three to four weeks. That's the window most lactation guidance points to, and it's shorter than many parents fear.

Why the wait? In the early weeks, your baby is learning to latch deeply, and your milk supply is being set by how often and how effectively baby feeds. A pacifier can stand in for a feed when baby is actually showing hunger cues, which can mean fewer feeds, less stimulation for your supply, and sometimes a shallower latch.

How do you know feeding is going well? Look for these signs:
- Baby latches comfortably and nursing isn't painful
- Steady wet and dirty diapers
- Weight gain on track at pediatric checks
- Your supply feels established

A few practical tips once you're there:
- Offer the pacifier after a feed or when baby is full but wants to suck, never to delay a feed.
- Watch for hunger cues first. Early cues like rooting and hand-to-mouth movements mean feed, don't soothe.
- Choose a one-piece, orthodontic-style design and keep it clean.

Some babies need different timing. If yours is premature, jaundiced, underweight, or struggling to latch, check with your pediatrician or a lactation consultant before introducing one.

And please don't spiral if you gave one early. I did, on day two with my second daughter, after a 3 a.m. meltdown and about 30 hours awake. She still nursed beautifully.
