A 100.4 Fever in a Newborn Means Go In, Not Wait

A NICU nurse explains what to do in the first hour of a newborn fever: how to take the temp, what to skip, and what the ER visit looks like.

Words by Melissa Torres · November 25, 2026

If your baby is under 3 months old and a rectal temperature reads 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, you go to the emergency department now. You don't wait for morning, you don't text a friend, and you don't give Tylenol first.

I know this cold, and it still got me. At 3:10 a.m. on a Tuesday, my youngest, 19 days old, read 100.6 on the rectal thermometer from our bathroom drawer. My hands shook so badly I took it a second time to be sure. We were in the car in six minutes, and I was calm by the time we parked, because the plan was already written in my head. That plan is what I want you to have.

Fever in a newborn is rarely the illness itself. It's the alarm, and in babies this young it can be the only alarm. Their immune systems are still learning the job, so a bacterial infection can look like a sleepy, slightly warm baby for hours. The American Academy of Pediatrics 2021 clinical practice guideline on febrile infants exists because of that uncertainty. Calm and fast are not opposites here. Calm is how you get fast.

Take the temperature the right way, once

For a baby under 3 months, use a digital rectal thermometer. Forehead scanners and ear thermometers are fine for toddlers, but in newborns they miss real fevers, and a falsely reassuring number is the most dangerous kind. Put a dab of petroleum jelly on the tip, slide it in about half an inch, and hold your baby's thigh steady until it beeps.

If your baby is swaddled in two blankets and a hat, strip them to a diaper and wait 10 to 15 minutes before a recheck. That's the only reason to wait. If the second reading is still 100.4 or higher, you're done measuring. Don't keep re-taking it hoping the number drops.

The first hour, in order

Here's the sequence I'd follow, and the one I teach families on our unit:

  • Write down the temperature and the time on your phone.
  • Leave the acetaminophen in the cabinet. It can mask the fever curve the team needs to read, and dosing under 12 weeks is a prescriber's call, not yours.
  • Call your pediatrician's after-hours line from the car, or while someone else drives. Don't sit waiting for a callback.
  • Grab the birth details: gestational age, your group B strep result, any antibiotics you had in labor, and the last feeding times.

Go to the emergency department, ideally one with pediatric staff. Tell the triage nurse the number and the age in your first sentence: "My baby is 19 days old and had a rectal temp of 100.6." What the ER will do, and why it's not overkill

Expect blood work, a urine sample collected by catheter, and sometimes a lumbar puncture. It looks aggressive when your baby is 3 weeks old and screaming. But in the large PECARN study published in JAMA Pediatrics in 2019, roughly 2 in 100 febrile infants under 60 days had bacteria in the blood or spinal fluid, and urinary infections were far more common than that. You can't spot those at the bedside. You can only test.

The AAP guideline splits babies into age bands, 8 to 21 days, 22 to 28 days, and 29 to 60 days, and the youngest get the most thorough workup and are usually admitted. Older babies who look well and have reassuring labs may go home. I'll be honest about the debate: clinicians still argue over how often a lumbar puncture is needed in the middle band, and your hospital may do it differently than the next one. Ask why, but don't refuse the workup because it feels like a lot.

What a fever isn't, and the panic I'd skip

Fussiness alone isn't a fever. That 6 p.m. screaming session is usually the pattern I wrote about in why the newborn witching hour is normal and you can't fix it, and a thermometer will settle the question in under a minute. A weak, high-pitched or inconsolable cry is a different thing, and what your newborn's cry actually means covers that distinction. And a temperature below 97.5°F can matter just as much as a high one in a baby this small, so treat it the same way and call.

Okay, a confession. With my middle kid I once took her temperature four times in ten minutes, and the thermometer read 96.8 each time because I'd put the battery cover on wrong and, I think, never fully turned it on? I'm a NICU nurse. I still stood in the hallway at midnight reading a manual. I'm not 100% sure I'd have caught it without my husband laughing at me, so please buy a spare battery and test the thing when you're not scared.

Tonight, find your thermometer, put a fresh battery in it, and tape a sticky note inside the cabinet door that says "100.4 rectal = go to the ER" along with the address of the nearest children's emergency department. At 3 a.m., you won't want to think. You'll want to read.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature is a fever in a newborn?

A rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months is a fever and needs emergency evaluation. Use a digital rectal thermometer, since forehead and ear readings are unreliable at this age.

Can I give my newborn Tylenol for a fever?

Not before a doctor has seen your baby. Under 12 weeks, acetaminophen should only be given on a clinician's instruction, and it can blur the picture the emergency team is trying to read.

Should I call the pediatrician first or go straight to the ER?

Go, and call on the way. Don't wait at home for a callback, because the hospital will do the same workup either way and the delay is the real risk.
