How to Swaddle Correctly, and When You Need to Stop

By Melissa Torres

Photo by Prabhash Kumar / Pexels

In my twelve years working in the NICU, I've wrapped thousands of newborns. Not hundreds. Thousands. And the one thing I can tell you with absolute certainty is that a good swaddle is one of the most effective calming tools a new parent has, and one of the most misused. I've watched well-meaning parents wrap their babies so tightly that hip dysplasia becomes a real concern, and I've seen others do it so loosely that the blanket becomes a hazard. Neither extreme helps anyone.

My position is simple: swaddling done correctly is genuinely beneficial for newborns in the first weeks of life, but it has a clear expiration date. Most parents keep going too long. The goal of this article isn't to scare you off swaddling or push you toward any particular product. It's to give you a technique that actually works, a reason it works, and a clear signal for when to move on.

Why Swaddling Works, and What It's Actually Doing

Newborns are born with the Moro reflex, sometimes called the startle reflex. It's that sudden full-body jerk you see when your baby hears a loud noise or feels like they're falling. It's completely normal, but it wakes them up constantly, especially during light sleep. A snug swaddle dampens that reflex by keeping the arms contained. That's the whole mechanism. It's not magic. It's just physics meeting neurology.

A 2017 review published in Pediatrics found that swaddled infants fell asleep faster and had longer sleep periods compared to unswaddled infants, though the same review flagged that improper technique significantly raised risk factors. So the tool works. The technique is what separates helpful from harmful. Knowing that distinction changed how I taught new parents in the unit, and how I swaddled my own three kids at home.

There's a common misconception that swaddling mimics the womb because it's warm and snug. That's partly true, but the compression around the arms is really the active ingredient. Babies who are wrapped only around the torso without containing the arms still startle awake. And a swaddle that's tight around the hips actively works against healthy hip development. You need both things at once: snug on top, loose on the bottom.

The Step-by-Step Technique That Actually Holds

Use a square muslin blanket, somewhere around 44 by 44 inches. Lay it flat in a diamond shape, fold the top corner down about six inches, and place your baby so their neck rests on that fold. Their shoulders should be just below the folded edge. This matters because you don't want fabric near their face.

Bring one arm down alongside their body, slightly bent at the elbow. Pull the same-side corner of the blanket across their chest and tuck it firmly under their opposite side. Not loosely draped. Tucked. Then fold the bottom of the blanket up over their feet, making sure there's plenty of room for the legs to bend and move freely. The hips should be able to frog out if you press gently. If they can't, the bottom is too tight. Finally, bring the remaining corner across, wrap it around, and tuck it at the back or side.

The test I always use: you should be able to slip two fingers between the blanket and your baby's chest, and the legs should have obvious room to flex. If you can't do both of those things simultaneously, rewrap. At home with my youngest, I'd do a quick two-finger check every single time before putting her down. Takes three seconds. Worth every one of them.

The Mistakes Parents Make Most Often

The biggest mistake is tight legs. I genuinely can't say this enough. The International Hip Dysplasia Institute has been clear for years that swaddling with the hips and knees extended in a straight position can contribute to hip dysplasia, especially in babies who already have some predisposition. And yet most swaddle tutorials you find online show exactly that: a baby wrapped like a burrito from shoulders to toes with no room to move. Don't do it.

The second mistake is continuing to swaddle after signs of rolling emerge. I've seen parents think that because swaddling helps their baby sleep, it must still be the right call. But once a baby starts showing any rolling motion, a swaddle with arms contained becomes a suffocation risk. Full stop. There's no workaround here.

And the third mistake, which people don't talk about enough, is using swaddle blankets that are too thin and slippery. They unravel. A loose blanket near a sleeping infant's face is exactly what safe sleep guidelines exist to prevent. Either use a blanket with enough weight to stay tucked, or use a zip-up swaddle sack designed for the purpose. Both work. A flimsy receiving blanket does not.

Knowing When to Stop: The Signs Are Clearer Than You Think

Most babies are ready to transition out of swaddling somewhere between 8 and 12 weeks, though some hit that threshold closer to 16 weeks. The number matters less than the behavior. The sign you're looking for is any attempt to roll. The moment your baby pushes up on one shoulder, tries to flip during tummy time, or rolls even partially during sleep, the swaddle comes off that night. Not gradually. That night.

A second sign is when a swaddle that previously calmed your baby starts agitating them instead. Babies around 10 to 12 weeks often begin needing their hands for self-soothing, bringing fingers to their mouth, rubbing their face. When swaddling fights that instinct, you'll know. They'll work hard to break free, and they'll be more unsettled swaddled than not. Trust what you're seeing.

The transition can feel daunting because you're taking away something that worked. One approach that I've recommended to parents and used myself is the one-arm-out method: keep one arm wrapped for a few nights, then both arms free. It eases the adjustment for babies who are used to the containment. It doesn't work for every baby, and that's okay. Some kids just do better with a cold-turkey transition to a sleep sack. Both paths get you to the same place.

Today, before your baby's next nap, do the two-finger chest check and the hip-flex test on your current swaddle. If either one fails, rewrap. And if your baby is anywhere near 8 weeks and you've noticed any shoulder rolling, put the swaddle away and try a wearable sleep sack tonight. You might be surprised how smoothly it goes.
