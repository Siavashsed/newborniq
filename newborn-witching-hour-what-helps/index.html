The Newborn Witching Hour Is Normal, and You Can't Fix It

Why newborns melt down every evening, what the crying curve research shows, and what actually lowers the volume, from a NICU nurse and mom of three.

Words by Melissa Torres · July 17, 2026

The newborn witching hour is real, it peaks around six weeks of age, and it is not caused by anything you're doing wrong. With my third baby, I spent most of March 2021 bouncing on a $25 yoga ball in my kitchen from 5:30 to 9:00 every night while my older two ate cereal for dinner because I couldn't put her down long enough to cook. I'd been a NICU nurse for over a decade at that point, and I still texted my own pediatrician one Tuesday to ask if something was wrong. Nothing was. So here's my position after three kids and a lot of night shifts: you cannot fix the witching hour, and trying to fix it usually makes it worse. You can only lower the volume and outlast it.

Pediatricians have known the shape of this pattern for a long time. In a classic 1962 study of 80 infants, T. Berry Brazelton charted daily crying and found it climbs from birth, peaks around six weeks at roughly two to three hours a day, and clusters heavily in the evening block, a finding the American Academy of Pediatrics still leans on today. The curve then falls on its own by three to four months. That timeline matters more than any single trick I'm going to give you.

The Crying Curve Runs on a Schedule, and Evenings Are It

Newborn crying isn't random noise. It follows a predictable developmental arc that researchers call the Period of PURPLE Crying, a framework from the National Center on Shaken Baby Syndrome. The P stands for peak, the U for unexpected, and the E at the end for evening, because that's when the bulk of it lands. A baby who was content all afternoon can start winding up at 5 p.m. for no visible reason. That's the pattern working as designed, not a symptom.

In my NICU work, this is part of discharge teaching for a reason. Parents who don't know the curve exists tend to read week-six evening crying as a sign that something broke... a supply problem, a formula problem, reflux, an allergy. Parents who know the curve read it as a phase with an expiration date. Same baby, same crying, completely different household.

And the crying itself carries less information in the evening than at other times of day. A hunger cry at 10 a.m. usually means hunger. A 6 p.m. cry is often just accumulated static. If you want to get better at telling those apart, I broke down what a newborn's different cries actually mean in an earlier piece.

Why Your Baby Falls Apart at 6 p.m. Specifically

Three things stack up by evening. First, newborns don't have a body clock yet. A 2015 study in PLOS ONE tracking salivary cortisol found that infants don't develop a mature circadian cortisol rhythm until somewhere around 8 to 12 weeks. Your baby's stress hormones literally don't know it's nighttime. Yours do, which is part of why the mismatch feels so brutal at exactly the hour you're most depleted.

Second, stimulation accumulates. Every face, light, errand, and older sibling shriek deposits a little arousal into a nervous system that can't discharge it yet. By dinnertime the tank is full. Third, for breastfed babies, milk flow tends to slow in the evening, so babies cluster feed... short, frequent, fussy sessions that feel like failure but are actually your baby placing tomorrow's milk order. Cluster feeding in the evening is normal biology, not evidence of low supply. For more on this, see Newborn Sleep Cycles in Week One: What's Actually Normal (And What Isn't).

None of this means your baby is overtired in the fixable sense. Week-one and week-two sleep is its own strange country, and I covered what's actually normal in first-week newborn sleep separately. The short version: a baby this age can be both exhausted and unable to sleep, and that's a maturity problem, not a scheduling problem.

What Actually Lowers the Volume

Since you can't cancel the witching hour, the goal is to shrink it. What worked across three kids and a lot of families I've coached, roughly in order of payoff:

  • Start the wind-down at 5 p.m., before the meltdown. Feed, dim the lights, close the blinds, put the phone away. Once the screaming starts you've lost your window.
  • Motion plus sound plus dark, all at once. A carrier walk in a dim hallway with white noise beats any single trick. Layered input works because it drowns the static rather than adding to it.
  • Accept the cluster feed. Park on the couch with water and a show at 6 p.m. instead of fighting for a schedule.
  • Run shifts. One adult takes 5 to 7, the other takes 7 to 9. Two half-broken parents beat two fully broken ones.
  • Put the baby down and step away when you're at your edge. A crying baby in a bassinet is safe. The NIH Safe to Sleep program is blunt about this, and so am I: five minutes in another room is good parenting, not giving up.

My friend Dana swore the hair dryer trick saved her, so one night I propped mine on the kitchen counter and let it run next to the bouncer. It worked for maybe eleven minutes, then tripped the breaker, and I stood in a dark kitchen holding a screaming baby, laughing in a way that probably sounded slightly unhinged. Honestly, I'm not sure the hair dryer deserved the credit anyway... it might have just been the eleven minutes. So take my ranking with that grain of salt.

Gear matters less than people think, but a decent carrier and a real white noise machine earn their keep at this hour more than anything else in the nursery. I put the few things I'd actually spend money on in this July's list of newborn products worth buying.

What Doesn't Work, and When to Actually Call Someone

Gas drops are the big one. Simethicone is safe, cheap, and heavily marketed, and randomized trials going back to a 1994 report in Pediatrics found it performed no better than placebo for infant crying. If it seems to work at your house, fine, it won't hurt. But don't let a $9 bottle convince you the crying was ever a gas problem.

The other trap is the supply panic. Evening fussiness at the breast drives more unnecessary formula top-ups than almost anything else I've seen, and with my second baby I nearly fell into it myself, pumping at 8 p.m. to "check" my supply and getting two ounces of proof that meant nothing, because pump output at that hour is always low. In most cases the fix is information, not formula. For more on this, see Building a Daily Routine With a Newborn That Isn't Miserable.

Now the honest boundary, without drama. Call your pediatrician the same day for a rectal temperature of 100.4 F or higher in a baby under three months, a cry that turns high-pitched or weak and stays that way, refusal to feed across multiple sessions, or inconsolable crying that runs past three hours and doesn't sound like your baby. Those are different animals from the witching hour, and you'll usually feel the difference before you can name it. Trust that feeling in both directions.

Set one alarm on your phone for 5:00 p.m. today, labeled with something kinder than "baby." When it goes off, feed the baby, dim the whole house, start the white noise, and strap on the carrier before the first wail, then hand off to your partner at a pre-agreed time. You won't erase tonight's witching hour. But starting fifteen minutes ahead of it, instead of ten minutes behind, is the single change that made my third baby's evenings survivable, and it costs nothing.

Frequently asked questions

What time is the newborn witching hour?

Most babies fuss hardest somewhere between 5 p.m. and 11 p.m., with the worst stretch often landing right around dinnertime. It typically starts around week two or three, peaks near six weeks, and fades by three to four months.

Is evening cluster feeding a sign my milk supply is low?

No. Frequent, fussy evening feeds are normal newborn behavior and actually signal your body to make more milk. Judge supply by wet diapers and weight gain at checkups, not by evening fussiness or pump output at night.

When is evening crying not normal?

Call your pediatrician for a rectal temperature of 100.4 F or higher in a baby under three months, a weak or high-pitched cry that persists, feeding refusal across several sessions, or inconsolable crying lasting more than three hours. Predictable evening fussing that responds at least partly to holding, feeding, and motion is the normal kind.
