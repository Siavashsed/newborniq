One RSV Antibody Shot Changed Our Winter Ward in 2024

Nirsevimab's first full RSV season in 2024: what the data showed, what parents asked, and what a NICU nurse thinks it means for newborn care.

Words by Melissa Torres · April 01, 2024

The biggest newborn care story of 2024 wasn't a gadget or a guideline. It was a single injection, nirsevimab (sold as Beyfortus), and its first full RSV season in the United States. I worked a night shift in January where a five-week-old came in at 3 a.m. sitting at 84% on room air, and her mom had spent two days being told by relatives that it was just a cold. Two beds down that same winter, I'd have been seeing a lot more of those babies, and I noticed I wasn't.

That's anecdote, and I know it. So I want to lay out what the numbers say, what parents actually asked us at the bedside, and where I think the rollout still falls short. My position is simple: this was the most useful new tool for newborns in years, and too many families still hear about it too late.

What happened when the shot finally reached babies

RSV is the leading cause of hospitalization in US infants, and for decades all we had was palivizumab, a monthly injection reserved for the highest-risk babies. In 2023 the CDC recommended a one-time dose of nirsevimab for all infants under 8 months entering their first RSV season, and the FDA approved a maternal vaccine, Abrysvo, for pregnancy weeks 32 through 36. You can read the current guidance on the CDC's RSV pages.

The rollout was messy. Supply ran short in the fall of 2023, and the CDC asked clinics to prioritize babies under 6 months and those at higher risk. By early 2024 most hospitals I know of had caught up. Some parents had driven to three pediatric offices to find a dose.

So 2024 was the first winter we could judge it at scale. Not perfectly, but at scale.

What the evidence says, and where it gets fuzzy

The pre-licensure trial, MELODY, found nirsevimab cut medically attended RSV lower respiratory infections by 74.5% in healthy infants, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2022. Early real-world estimates from the CDC's 2023-24 surveillance, reported in MMWR, landed around 90% against RSV hospitalization. That's a striking number.

Here's the caveat I'd give any parent. Early estimates come with wide confidence intervals, and the shot protects against severe RSV, not every sniffle. A protected baby can still catch RSV. They're just far less likely to end up on oxygen. I've watched families hear "RSV protection" and assume "no RSV," and then panic at the first cough.

It's an antibody, not a vaccine

Nirsevimab hands the baby ready-made antibodies. It doesn't train the immune system, which is why it lasts about five months and why it's one dose. That distinction calmed a surprising number of worried parents once someone said it plainly.

The questions parents actually asked me

Almost nobody asked about efficacy. They asked whether it was safe, whether it was new, and whether they'd get billed. On safety, the trial side effects were mostly a sore leg and a brief rash. On cost, most insurers cover it and the Vaccines for Children program covers eligible families. I'd still tell you to call your plan before discharge day, not on it.

Okay, a looser moment. My friend Dani swore she'd never let anyone "experiment" on her baby, then spent forty minutes in my kitchen reading the fact sheet out loud while her toddler ate a crayon. She booked the shot the next morning. I'm not 100% sure what changed her mind, honestly, but I think it was just someone she trusted saying the words "I gave it to my own kids' cousin" without any sales voice. I felt faintly ridiculous being that person.

And that's the thing... trust did more work than any handout we printed.

Why timing, not hesitancy, is the real gap

Everyone worried about refusal in 2024. In my experience the bigger problem was calendar luck. A baby born in March missed the window. A baby born in October got the dose in the hospital. A baby born in August, whose parents were never told about the maternal vaccine at 32 weeks, fell through the middle.

That's why I'd change one thing about how we talk to families:

  • Bring it up at the prenatal visit, by 28 to 32 weeks, so parents can choose between the maternal vaccine and the infant shot.
  • Confirm before discharge if the baby is born between October and March.
  • Don't double up on purpose. If the mother had Abrysvo at least 14 days before delivery, the baby usually doesn't need nirsevimab.

This also connects to how we think about the whole first season at home. A newborn with a protected respiratory season is easier to build a rhythm around, which I covered in building a daily routine with a newborn that isn't miserable. And the conversations we now fold into discharge, from RSV to sleep, are the same ones I described in how the 2024 safe sleep change rewrote NICU discharge talks.

What 2024 settled, and what it didn't

It settled that a population-wide RSV prevention tool for infants works outside a trial. It didn't settle how to make access equal. Rural clinics, families without regular pediatric care, and babies born just outside the season all got less of the benefit.

I made my own mistake that winter too. I assumed a mom who'd had a smooth pregnancy had been told about the maternal vaccine. She hadn't. I only found out at 2 a.m. on day two, and by then the choice was already narrowed. Now I ask first.

Where I land: don't wait for your pediatrician to bring it up. The shot is good. The system around it is still catching up.

At your next prenatal visit, ask one question: "Which RSV protection should we plan for, and when?" Write down the answer, and if your baby is due between October and March, confirm it again at the hospital before you leave.

Frequently asked questions

Does my baby need the RSV shot if I got the RSV vaccine while pregnant?

Usually not. If you received Abrysvo at 32 to 36 weeks and delivered at least 14 days later, your baby is generally considered protected. Your pediatrician may still recommend the infant shot in specific situations, such as a late or uncertain vaccination.

When is RSV season for newborns in the US?

In most of the continental US it runs roughly October through March. Babies born in that window can get nirsevimab in the first week of life, while babies born earlier are typically dosed in the fall.

Is nirsevimab a vaccine?

No. It's a monoclonal antibody, so it gives your baby protection directly instead of training their immune system. That's why it works right away and lasts about five months.
