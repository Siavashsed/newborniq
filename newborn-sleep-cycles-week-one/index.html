Newborn Sleep Cycles in Week One: What's Actually Normal (And What Isn't)

By Melissa Torres

Photo by Prabhash Kumar / Pexels

Most parents come home from the hospital expecting their baby to sleep a lot. And technically, newborns do sleep a lot. Somewhere between 16 and 18 hours a day in the first week, according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2020 guidelines. But here's what nobody warns you about: those hours are scattered across the entire 24-hour clock in chunks of 45 minutes to two hours, with no regard whatsoever for whether it's 2 p.m. or 2 a.m. That's not a problem you need to fix. That's normal newborn biology doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

My position on this is pretty firm, and I'll tell you why. After nearly a decade as a NICU nurse and three kids of my own, I've watched exhausted parents spiral into anxiety because their week-old baby doesn't sleep in long, predictable stretches. They're convinced something is wrong. Almost always, nothing is wrong. What they're missing is a basic understanding of how a newborn's sleep is actually structured, and once they have that, a lot of the fear dissolves. So that's what I want to give you here.

Why Newborn Sleep Looks Nothing Like Adult Sleep

A full-term newborn spends roughly 50 percent of their sleep time in active sleep, which is the developmental equivalent of REM sleep in adults. During active sleep, you'll see eye fluttering, twitching limbs, little grunts, and facial expressions that look almost like smiling or grimacing. New parents often interpret this as waking up, or being uncomfortable. It's neither. It's the brain doing serious construction work, building neural connections at a rate that won't happen again in a human lifetime.

The other half of sleep is quiet sleep, which is deeper and calmer. The problem is that newborns cycle through these two states every 45 to 50 minutes, and they're very easy to rouse at the transition point between cycles. That's the moment when you've finally put the baby down and they jolt awake the second you step back. They weren't fully asleep yet. They were transitioning, and the shift in position or temperature broke the cycle before it could deepen.

This architecture isn't a design flaw. Active sleep is theorized to protect young infants from prolonged oxygen deprivation by keeping them closer to arousal. Research from 2018 published in Current Biology found that active sleep in infants plays a direct role in motor system development, which is why that twitching matters. The sleep looks chaotic. It's not.

The First 48 Hours Are Their Own Category

In my work with newborns in the NICU, I've noticed that the first 48 hours after birth follow a pattern that's somewhat distinct from the rest of week one. Most babies are alert and relatively wakeful in the first hour or two after delivery. Then they crash. Hard. That long sleep in the first day can last anywhere from four to six hours and it sometimes worries parents who are trying to establish breastfeeding. It's normal recovery from the stress of being born.

After that initial crash, babies often enter what lactation consultants call the "second night" phase, usually around 24 to 48 hours, where they seem to want to nurse constantly and sleep very little. This is not a supply problem. It's cluster feeding, and it's the baby's way of signaling the body to ramp up milk production. It feels relentless. It usually resolves within a night or two.

So when I say week one is its own thing, I mean it. Days three through seven tend to settle into slightly more recognizable patterns, with sleep windows of one to three hours becoming more consistent. But consistent doesn't mean predictable by the clock. Don't expect that yet.

The Biggest Misconception Parents Bring Home

The one thing I'd genuinely push back on is the idea that a newborn who won't sleep flat on their back in a bassinet is somehow broken or difficult. I hear this constantly. Parents say their baby only sleeps when held, and they're worried they're creating a bad habit at six days old. I want to be direct: you cannot spoil a newborn. Contact sleep feels safe to them because it mimics the womb environment they just left. Warmth, heartbeat, movement. Of course they prefer it.

The safety piece still matters, and I'm not dismissing it. The AAP's safe sleep guidelines are there for real reasons, and I follow them with my own kids. But the goal in week one isn't sleep training. It's survival, recovery, and feeding. If your baby is sleeping in two-hour chunks, eating, and having wet diapers, you're doing it right even if it doesn't feel that way at midnight.

There's genuine debate in pediatric circles about when and whether to start any kind of sleep shaping, with some practitioners suggesting gentle wake windows as early as six to eight weeks, and others recommending waiting until 12 weeks or later. That debate doesn't touch week one at all. Week one is too early. Full stop.

What You Can Actually Do This Week to Make It Easier

When I had my second child, I was prepared for the sleep deprivation but I still underestimated one thing: my own need to watch the baby sleep. I'd lie there listening for every sound instead of sleeping when she did. It's incredibly common and incredibly counterproductive. The single most useful thing you can do in week one isn't a sleep strategy for the baby. It's a rest strategy for yourself. Sleep in shifts with a partner if you have one. Accept help when it's offered. Lower every non-essential standard you have for what this week needs to look like.

For the baby, keeping the environment calm and dim during nighttime feeds, and brighter and more stimulating during daytime awake windows, does start to lay very early groundwork for day and night differentiation. Newborns don't have a developed circadian rhythm at birth. That starts developing around six to eight weeks. But light exposure is one of the cues that helps calibrate it over time, so starting the habit early doesn't hurt.

Track feeds and sleep loosely, not obsessively. An app or a simple notebook is fine. You're looking for broad patterns and making sure the baby is feeding eight to twelve times in 24 hours, not trying to enforce a schedule. If wake windows are stretching past two hours and the baby seems distressed rather than alert and content, that's worth mentioning to your pediatrician. But most of the time, in most cases, what you're watching is just a brand-new nervous system figuring itself out.

The one thing you can take from this today: put the milestone expectations down for the next seven days. Your newborn's sleep isn't broken, and you don't need to fix it. What you need is accurate information about what's actually happening in that small, busy brain, and permission to stop measuring week one against a standard that won't apply for months. Sleep will change. It always does. But it changes on the baby's biological timeline, not ours, and accepting that early makes the whole first week significantly more bearable.
