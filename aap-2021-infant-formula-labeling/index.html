The AAP's 2021 Formula Labeling Push Changed What Parents Could Actually Trust

The AAP's 2021 call to overhaul infant formula labeling mattered more than most parents realized. Here's what it meant and still means.

Words by Melissa Torres · August 18, 2021

The American Academy of Pediatrics published a policy statement in June 2021 that most mainstream parenting outlets buried under diaper bag reviews. That was a mistake. The statement, "Optimizing Nutrition in Infants and Young Children," called out a problem that I had watched play out on NICU floors for years: formula labeling in the United States was, and largely still is, a mess of marketing language dressed up as nutritional guidance. I remember standing at the bedside of a 34-week preemie in early 2021, watching a mother hold three different formula cans trying to figure out which one was appropriate for a transitioning premature infant. The labels all said things like "supports brain development" and "clinically proven." None of them clearly explained the caloric density or the protein-to-whey ratio she actually needed to ask about. She wasn't uninformed. The labels just weren't written for her.

The AAP's 2021 statement didn't land like a bombshell in the press. But inside pediatric circles, it started a conversation that I'd argue was overdue by at least a decade. The core argument was straightforward: parents were being sold outcomes, not information. And the research backing that concern was harder to ignore than it used to be.

What the AAP Actually Said, Without the Spin

The 2021 policy statement did several things at once. It pushed back against the explosion of "specialty" infant formulas marketed for colic, reflux, and sleep, noting that the clinical evidence for most of them was thin at best. It also flagged that parents were spending significantly more money on these products without proportionally better outcomes. A 2021 analysis in Nutrients found that specialty formula products had grown to represent nearly 35% of U.S. formula sales despite limited peer-reviewed support for most of their added-ingredient claims.

What the AAP asked for was clearer, standardized language on labels so that caregivers, including nurses like me, could quickly parse what was actually in the can versus what the marketing team wanted you to believe was in the can. That sounds basic. It isn't, when you factor in the lobbying power of the formula industry and the FDA's historically cautious approach to regulating health claims on food products.

The statement also addressed toddler formulas specifically, which had proliferated wildly and were being positioned on store shelves right next to infant formulas. That proximity alone was causing confusion. The AAP's position: most toddler formulas were unnecessary and the labeling made them look more medically endorsed than they were.

Why 2021 Was the Right Moment for This Fight

Timing matters in policy. The 2021 statement dropped during a period when formula was already under a microscope. The pandemic had disrupted supply chains, parents were stress-buying and stockpiling, and online communities were sharing formula-switching advice at a speed that worried neonatologists. Incorrect formula substitutions in very young infants aren't harmless. I've seen it firsthand, particularly in households where a perceived shortage led to watering down or switching to a formula with the wrong macronutrient profile for the baby's age or corrected gestational age. Against that backdrop, the AAP's call for better labeling was less abstract than it might have seemed in a quieter year. CDC Breastfeeding Report Card data showed that as of 2021, approximately 83% of U.S. infants were given some formula in the first year of life, meaning the stakes of misleading labeling were population-scale, not niche.

And the FDA's track record on this was genuinely complicated. The agency had regulatory authority but had historically moved slowly on formula health claim enforcement, partly because the 1980 Infant Formula Act set a floor for safety standards without doing much about marketing language. The 2021 AAP statement was, in part, a public nudge to the FDA to use authority it already technically had.

The Industry Response and Where the Gaps Still Lived

I want to be honest here, because I think some of the coverage of this issue oversimplified things. Formula companies didn't just ignore the AAP statement, and a few of them had already been moving toward cleaner label language before 2021. Similac had reformulated some products, and Enfamil had updated some of its on-can claims language in response to earlier FTC inquiries. So it wasn't a clean "industry bad, AAP good" story.

Where it got genuinely murky was in the middle tier of smaller brands, many of them European imports that were being sold through online retailers without going through the same FDA notification process that domestic manufacturers faced. Parents were buying these products, sometimes paying $40 to $60 per can, based on Instagram recommendations and the (often accurate) perception that U.S. formula standards lagged behind European ones. And here's the thing... that perception wasn't entirely wrong. European formulas do face stricter restrictions on added sugars and certain additives. But "stricter in some ways" doesn't mean "automatically better for your specific baby," and the labeling on imported products was even harder to interpret than domestic ones, sometimes literally because it was in German.

I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone, but my friend Diane, who's also a NICU nurse at a different hospital, started putting together a one-page formula comparison sheet for her unit's discharge parents around mid-2021 because the questions had gotten so frequent and so specific. She emailed it to me and honestly, it was better than anything the formula companies had produced. Which is kind of embarrassing when you think about it. What This Meant for Families Navigating Newborn Feeding Right Now

If you had a newborn in 2021, this policy shift probably didn't change your shopping experience overnight. Labels didn't magically improve. But what the AAP statement did was give pediatricians and nurses clearer language to push back with when a parent came in asking whether they should switch to a "brain-supporting" formula that cost twice as much. Before 2021, some providers hedged. After the statement, there was an official position to point to.

For parents trying to build any kind of feeding routine in those first weeks, the noise around formula choices was genuinely exhausting. If you've read our piece on building a daily routine with a newborn that isn't miserable, you know that predictability matters enormously in those early weeks. Constantly second-guessing the formula in the bottle doesn't help that. Having a pediatrician who can confidently say "this standard formula is appropriate for your full-term baby" because they're backed by clear AAP guidance is worth more than any label claim.

The 2021 AAP statement also landed in the same year as significant FDA action on sleep products. If you want the full picture of how 2021 shaped newborn product safety broadly, the FDA's 2021 infant sleep product warning belongs in that same conversation. These weren't isolated events. They were part of a wider push to hold the newborn product market to a higher standard of evidence.

  • Standard term infant formula: doesn't need a specialty label to be appropriate for most healthy newborns
  • "Comfort" or "gentle" formulas: evidence for reflux or colic benefit is limited per the AAP's own review
  • Toddler formulas: the AAP explicitly called these unnecessary for most children over 12 months who eat a varied diet
  • Imported European formulas: may have stricter additive rules but aren't FDA-reviewed and labels can be harder to parse

The conversation the AAP started in 2021 hasn't fully resolved. The safe sleep rule changes in 2024 showed that these policy updates take years to fully move from statement to standard practice. Formula labeling is no different. But knowing the starting point matters.

Pull up your baby's current formula can and find the ingredient list. Ignore the front-panel claims entirely. If you're ever uncertain whether it's the right match for your baby's age and feeding needs, bring that can to your next pediatric appointment and ask your provider to walk through the label with you. That's not overthinking it. That's exactly the kind of question the 2021 AAP statement was designed to make easier to answer. Frequently asked questions

Did the AAP 2021 statement change what formula is sold in stores?

Not immediately. The statement was a policy position urging the FDA and manufacturers to improve labeling standards, not a regulatory rule change. Formula products on shelves in 2021 were largely unchanged, but the statement gave pediatricians a firmer foundation to guide families away from unnecessary specialty products.

Are specialty or comfort formulas actually worth buying?

For most healthy full-term infants, no. The AAP's 2021 review found limited clinical evidence supporting the added-ingredient claims on most comfort or colic formulas. If your baby has a diagnosed condition like MSPI or GERD, talk to your pediatrician about a medically appropriate formula rather than choosing by label language.

Are European infant formulas safer or better than U.S. formulas?

European formulas do face stricter restrictions on certain additives and added sugars under EU regulation. But they aren't reviewed by the FDA, so they don't meet the same notification and composition reporting requirements as domestic brands. Neither is automatically better for your baby. The right formula depends on your baby's specific needs, age, and any medical considerations.
